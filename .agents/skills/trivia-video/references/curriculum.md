# The teaching overlay

Read this alongside `SKILL.md` while Carrie is still learning. It is the *only* part of this skill
with an expiry date — the seven steps run forever, this file stops mattering somewhere around
video three. When it stops mattering, delete it. Nothing in `SKILL.md` depends on it.

**The split it exists to hold:** the seven steps are the *production*, unchanging and the same for
anyone. The sessions below are the *curriculum*, a one-time path through those steps for one
person. They are different lists on purpose. Sessions 1 and 3 do work that only happens once;
everything else recurs every video forever.

## The rules that govern every session

- **One new concept. Not two.** The named batch that must never arrive together is MCP servers,
  skills, CLI choice and model choice. Never name a concept until the capability it bought has
  already done something she asked for.
- **A session's goal is a task outcome, never understanding a technology.** "The AI can go look
  things up for you" is a lesson. "Understand MCP" is not.
- **End mid-appetite.** Every session closes with a named hook into the next one, never at a tidy
  stopping point. The hooks are written below; they are not optional decoration.
- **She gets something to look at, every single time.** Sessions 2, 3 and 4 produce nothing
  watchable, which is the exact stretch where impatience turns into a verdict about AI. A handback
  is not delivered when it is written to storage — it is delivered when it arrives in Telegram as
  a thing she can look at. Ten frames arrive as ten pictures. The screenplay arrives as text she
  reads on her phone. A filepath is not a handback.
- **Write a session report at the end**, and open the next session by letting her say where they
  are before you tell her.

**How you know you went too long:** she starts asking clarifying questions about the thing from
ten minutes ago instead of reacting to the thing in front of her. That is the overshoot signal,
and it arrives before she says anything about it. Stop there, even mid-step. A heuristic, not a
rule — but it is the best one available.

## The sessions

| # | She gets | The one concept | Spine steps | Board |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | An account of what makes these videos work | It can go and look — you gave it a way | *(research feeding DIRECT)* + **BRIEF** | card created, in **What it's about** |
| 2 | The question set, in a document she can open | It writes things down somewhere that outlives the chat | **SOURCE** + **CALIBRATE** | → **Picking the questions** |
| 3 | A screenplay and a look | Taste can be written down and reused | **DIRECT** | → **The look** |
| 4 | Ten pictures | It makes things — and whoever makes them is swappable | **GENERATE** (stills) | → **The pictures** |
| 5 | A whole video, and it is ugly | Get it end to end before making any of it good | **GENERATE** (voice) + **ASSEMBLE** d0 | → **The cut** |
| 6 | The same video, good | You are training a crew, not making one video | **ASSEMBLE** d1 | → **The cut** |
| 7 | A decision | You are the boss — the credit and the blame | **APPROVE** | → **Yours to post** |

### Session 1 — what makes these work

Ask what she has been watching and what made her want to make one. Then go find out properly: real
trivia videos, what performed, why. She wanted the answer, so the tool arrives as the obvious way
to get it — not as a topic. When you use web search, say what you are doing in plain words: *"I
can't answer this from memory, let me go look."* That sentence is the whole lesson.

**Close with BRIEF, and it is the hook:** *"So what do you want the first one to be about?"* She
has just spent an hour full of what works. She answers. That answer is the brief, the run slug,
and the thing she thinks about all week. Create the card with her topic as the title, and leave it.

### Session 2 — the questions

Open the board for the first time here, because this session's concept is persistence and the board
is the most visible instance of it. One card, her topic, sitting in a column. Do not explain the
board; let her see it. If she asks how it works, tell her properly — answering a question she
asked is never a violation.

SOURCE runs cheap and wide, and say so as you do it: *"using the cheap model for this bit, it's
just looking things up."* That single line, repeated across sessions, is what eventually lets her
answer "which model would you use to find questions, and why" without ever having sat through a
lesson on model routing.

CALIBRATE runs strong, confirms every answer, and writes the set somewhere she can open.
**Your doubt here is the most important one in the whole curriculum** — name the question you think
sits closest to the edge and which edge it is on. Her answer to that is her first real act of
direction.

**Hook:** *"Next time we figure out what it looks like."*

### Session 3 — the look

This is where `carries-house-style` gets written, and it gets written from her answers. Interview
her: what should it feel like, what does she hate, what has she seen that she wants nothing to do
with. Then write the skill, show it to her, and say plainly that this one is hers and it will be
read every time from now on.

The concept is that taste can be written down and reused. It lands because she just watched her own
answers become a thing with her name on it. Commit it to the repo in this session — a skill that
only exists in runtime state dies with the next rebuild.

**Hook:** *"Next time it actually draws them."*

### Session 4 — the pictures

Ten frames arrive in the chat as ten pictures. Frame 1 anchors the rest; say that out loud when you
do it, because "the first one sets the style for the others" is a fact about the work, not jargon.

This is the impatience session. Assets are piling up and there is still nothing to watch. Name the
next session before she has to wonder: *"next time this becomes an actual video."* Then be quick
about getting there.

**Hook:** *"Next time you watch the whole thing."*

### Session 5 — the ugly one

The highest-risk hour in the project. She is about to watch something bad, and the named total
misfire is that she generalizes from a bad video to a bad technology.

Say it is rough before she watches, in one line, without apologizing and without explaining a
theory of software development: *"this is the whole thing start to finish, and it's ugly on
purpose — I want you to see the shape before we make any of it nice."* Then shut up and let her
watch it.

When she reacts, listen for which one she says. *"This part is boring"* or *"I don't know how to
get what I want yet"* means it is working. *"AI is bad at this"* means the pacing was wrong, and
the fix is more of her decisions in the next pass, not more explanation of this one.

**Hook:** *"Next time we make it good, and you pick what gets fixed first."*

### Session 6 — making it good

She picks what gets fixed. Every addition lands on a cut that already plays end to end, and every
one of them is hers — say whose idea each change was as you make it, because the accumulating
count of *her* decisions is the entire mindset argument, delivered without ever being stated.

The concept is that she is training a crew, not making a video. It lands here because this is the
first time anything gets repeated.

**Hook:** *"Next time you decide whether it goes out."*

### Session 7 — hers

Hand it over. Say it is hers to post. Stop talking.

If she rejects it, that is the session succeeding, not failing. Ask what specifically, take the
redirect by name, and go back to that step.

## After the curriculum

Video two is not seven sessions. It is two or three, because the look is written down now and the
question process is known. Video three should be one conversation. The number of times she has to
tell you something should fall on every video — that decline *is* the product, more than any single
video is.

When it gets to one conversation, this file has done its job. Delete it. The seven steps carry on
without it, which was the point of keeping them separate.
